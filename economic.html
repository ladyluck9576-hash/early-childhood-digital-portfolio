Context 1: Economic Context - Child Poverty and Housing Stress

Understanding the Context

Economic hardship within the ECEC is frequently faced as unstable housing, lack of food, means of transport being stopped, uneven attendance and parents incurring rent, utility, health care and childcare at the expense of their counterparts. The new 202425 estimates indicate that in 202425 there were approximately 1.26 million households where the tipping point of housing stress was reached in Australia, with a median national rent of $681 per week in December 2025 (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare [AIHW], 2026). Li et al. (2024) conducted an analysis of 4,355 children aged four to five in Australia and found that inappropriate housing concentrated various disadvantages and led to lower functionalities in terms of emotional and psychosocial well-being. This recontextualizes suffering as material limits as opposed to parental failure.

Family Stress Model (Conger et al., 2010) is a model that describes how economic pressure may escalate caregiver distress and discord, decreasing the provision of emotionally available, consistent parenting; the processes described by Bronfenbrenner (1979) ecological perspective occur in housing markets, welfare settings, employment and neighbourhood resources. Modelling also reports that, with specific income supplementation in early-childhood, inequalities in social-emotional, physical and learning achievement may decrease and mental health of parents may increase (Goldfeld et al., 2024). There are differences in impacts on sole-parent, migrant, First Nation, disability-affected and high-rent households, grappling with varying costs, discrimination and service barriers. Teachers must then have questions rather than presumptions when it becomes hard to participate.

Impact on Children and Families

Housing and financial strain can affect development through linked pathways. Hunger and inadequate sleep impair attention and control; often changing locations disrupt peer connections and continuity; crowding disrupts solitary play; parental anxiety may enhance family conflict. This can manifest in ECEC as fatigue, food-seeking, separation-distress, withdrawal, aggression or inconsistent attendance, which are non-specific. Ethical provision does not involve surveillance and treatment as a social charity. The option of flexible involvement, in discrete material support and a consistent key educator can maintain belonging even as families seek specialist help.

Social Policy and Australian Responses

Housing and homelessness response is funded by the National Agreement on Social Housing and Homelessness (Department of Social Services [DSS], 2024), which becomes effective in July 2024, and the Early Years Strategy 20242034 (Australian Government Department of Education, 2024) focuses on empowered families, supportive communities and coordinated systems. Accessibility is restricted by eligibility criteria, waitlists, transportation and online administration. Improving policy-lived experience gaps can be achieved by routine social-needs conversations, warm referrals through consent and flexibility of fees or participation in ECEC services. Pedagogy can mitigate misfortune but not replacing the sufficient income and safe housing.

Strategies for Practice

1. Make confidential questions about food, transport, housing and costs at enrolment and transitions normal and reduce stigma and identify barriers at an early stage.
2. Safeguard continuity by having a regular key educator, routine images and shaping arrival routines where moves or transport disruption destabilise family life.
3. Cut down on the unseen expenses by offering common resources, field trip support and family parties without naming families that receive support.
4. Make warm referrals: get agreement by elucidating the service with permission, making contact and assenting to a follow-up rather than merely passing on details.
5. Educate in equity with narratives and collaborative experiences, not focusing on stereotypes of poverty, and never asking children to reveal family deprivation.

Community and Professional Partnerships

1. Services Australia social workers: link families to payments and crisis supports that are in place; through educators, contact can be made without disclosure control.
2. National Debt Helpline financial counsellors: free debt and bill advice before the financial squeeze sets in.
3. MH services: Housing and homelessness services: support tenancy risk, emergency accommodation, and case coordination and ECEC safeguards continuity.
4. Foodbank and local food-relief partners: enhance access to food using non-stigmatising, confidential programs.
5. Family support practitioners or social workers: coordinate housing, health, financial and parenting referrals across fragmented systems.

Resources for Educators and Children

Programs and websites

1. Ask Izzy: location-based housing, food and money support; search collaboratively with families.
2. Services Australia - Families: clarifies payments and supports; use privately during referral planning.
3. National Debt Helpline: explains free financial counselling; supports early, non-judgmental referral.
4. Foodbank Australia: locates food-relief pathways; pair access with confidentiality and dignity.

Children’s storybooks

1. Last Stop on Market Street, Matt de la Peña: explores community strengths beyond possessions.
2. A Chair for My Mother, Vera B. Williams: supports discussion of saving, loss and family cooperation.
3. Maddi’s Fridge, Lois Brandt: builds empathy around food insecurity while locating help with adults.
4. Fly Away Home, Eve Bunting: supports careful discussion of home, safety and hope with older preschoolers.

Videos and educational media

1. Sesame Workshop, A Rainbow Kind of Day: introduces homelessness through feelings, safety and trusted adults.
2. Sesame Workshop, Elmo’s Spend, Share, and Save Jars: develops age-appropriate ideas about choices and resources.
3. Sesame Workshop, Helping Others: models prosocial help that respects another person’s needs.
4. Sesame Workshop, Being a Good Neighbor: connects belonging with practical community care.
