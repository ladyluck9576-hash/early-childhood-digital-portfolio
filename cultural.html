Cultural & Diversity Context

Refugee, Migrant & Multilingual Families

Cultural responsiveness means recognising language, identity, migration history and family knowledge as strengths while addressing racism, institutional barriers, resettlement stress and unequal access.

27.6%of Australians were born overseas and 22.8% used a language other than English at home in the 2021 Census (ABS, 2022).

Understanding the Context

Cultural diversity in Australian ECEC is increasingly ordinary rather than exceptional. For refugee and migrant families, however, diversity can intersect with disrupted education, visa histories, resettlement stress, unfamiliar institutions, racism and loss of extended networks. A deficit lens that treats multilingualism or unfamiliarity with Australian systems as developmental risk obscures family knowledge, language resources and survival capacities.

Bernhardt et al.’s (2024) systematic review of children aged zero to six after forced displacement found risks across social-emotional, cognitive and language development, but outcomes varied with caregiver wellbeing, family functioning, living conditions and access to preschool and health supports. Displacement is therefore not a diagnosis. Intersectionality (Crenshaw, 1989) helps educators examine how migration status, race, language, gender, class and disability combine to create distinct barriers. Funds of knowledge theory (Moll et al., 1992) provides a complementary pedagogical lens: households hold culturally developed knowledge, practices and relationships that can enrich curriculum when educators learn with families.

McInnes et al. (2025) found that codesigned professional learning in diverse Australian birth-to-five settings could strengthen educators’ support for multilingual children’s social and emotional development. Cultural responsiveness therefore requires sustained relationship-building, not festival days or translated notices alone.

Impact on Children & Families

Children may be learning English while simultaneously maintaining a home language, interpreting unfamiliar routines and, for some, adapting after displacement or trauma. Misreading quiet observation as low ability, code-switching as confusion, or limited English as weak thinking can lower expectations. Parents may hesitate to ask questions if previous institutions were unsafe or if professional roles differ from those in their country of origin.

ECEC services need culturally safe transition processes, interpreters, visual communication and opportunities for family knowledge to shape curriculum. These approaches protect identity, belonging and equitable participation without expecting assimilation.

Social Policy and Australian Responses

Australia’s Humanitarian Program provides 20,000 places in 2025–26 (Department of Home Affairs, 2026), while the Humanitarian Settlement Program and Settlement Engagement and Transition Support program assist eligible newcomers with participation, education, English and community connection. Within ECEC, the Early Years Learning Framework V2.0 emphasises belonging, cultural responsiveness and partnerships (Australian Government Department of Education, 2022).

Closing the Gap also requires a distinct commitment to Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children; Target 4 seeks increased developmental progress across all five AEDC domains by 2031 (Australian Government, 2020). Migrant inclusion must not displace First Nations sovereignty and cultural safety. Policy becomes meaningful when enrolment systems accept varied documentation, interpreters are routine, racism is challenged explicitly and families participate in decisions.

Five Evidence-Based Strategies for Practice

1Use qualified interpreters
Use them for consequential conversations and consent; never place children in the role of translating sensitive information.
2Map cultural strengths
Ask about names, languages, routines, family goals, celebrations and preferred communication at enrolment.
3Sustain home languages
Use bilingual labels, songs, books, family-recorded stories and permission to use all linguistic resources during play.
4Audit curriculum for bias
Ensure representation across the year and avoid stereotypes, costumes or “tourist” approaches to culture.
5Scaffold transitions
Use photographs, visual schedules, predictable routines and gradual family participation.

Community & Professional Partnerships

TIS National interpreters
Accurate two-way communication for enrolment, consent, health and safeguarding discussions.
Settlement workers
SETS or Humanitarian Settlement Program workers can coordinate participation and education needs.
Refugee health nurses
Health, immunisation, development and navigation of health systems.
STARTTS / Foundation House
Specialist refugee trauma support and trauma-aware engagement advice.
Bicultural workers & community leaders
Strengthen trust and co-design culturally legitimate approaches without speaking for every family.
