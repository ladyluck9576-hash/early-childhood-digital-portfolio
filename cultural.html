Context 3: Cultural and Diversity Context - Refugee, Migrant and Multilingual Families

Understanding the Context

The Australian ECEC cultural diversity is becoming more of a normal than a special situation: 27.6 per cent of Australians were born abroad and 22.8 per cent spoke a non-English language at home in the 2021 Census (Australian Bureau of Statistics [ABS], 2022). In the case of refugee and migrant families, though, diversity can be compounded by the lack of education, visa records, resettlement strains, the inability to know institutions, racism and loss of extended family ties. A deficit lens treating multilingualism or unfamiliarity with systems of Australian development as risk of development obscures the knowledge of the family, language resources and survival abilities. A systematic review of children aged zero to six years conducted by Bernhardt et al. (2024) on children following forced displacement revealed risks in the social-emotional, cognitive and language development area, yet revealed that the outcomes varied according to wellbeing of the caregivers, family functioning, living conditions and access to preschool and health supports. It is not a diagnosis of displacement.

Intersectionality (Crenshaw, 1989) assists educators to analyze how the migration status, race, language, gender, class and disability can interact to produce specific obstacles and not a single refugee experience. The theory of funds of knowledge (Moll et al., 1992) also offers a valuable alternative approach to pedagogy: households possess culturally constructed knowledge, practices and relationships, which could be a valuable addition to the curriculum, when teachers do not just teach families Australian norms. McInnes et al. (2025) discovered that codesigned professional learning in different settings in Australia with diverse birth-to-five students may enhance the ability of educators to support social and emotional growth of multilingual children. Cultural responsiveness takes a long-term commitment of relationship-building, not just the days of festivals or translated notices.

Impact on Children and Families

Children can be acquiring English and retaining a mother tongue at the same time, trying to figure out new routines, and in some cases, adjusting to post-displacement or post-trauma. The expectations can be lowered due to misinterpretation of the low ability, confusion, or limited English as poor thinking. The parents, too, can be reluctant to pose questions when the past institutions were insecure or the professional roles are different than the ones in the country of origin. Culturally safe transition processes, interpreters, visual communication and family knowledge influence curriculum development are required of ECEC services.

Social Policy and Australian Responses

The Humanitarian Program offers 20,000 places in 2025 26 (Department of Home Affairs, 2026) and the Humanitarian Settlement Program and Settlement Engagement and Transition Support program assists eligible newcomers by assisting them in their participation, education, English, and community connection. In the ECEC, belonging, cultural responsiveness and partnerships are a focus within the Early Years Learning Framework V2.0 (Australian Government Department of Education, 2022). Another specific focus of closing the Gap is on Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children; Target 4 aims at greater progress in all five AEDC domains by 2031 (Australian Government, 2020). The following policy strands should not be conflated: migrant inclusion should not override First Nations sovereignty and cultural safety. Teachers require locally pertinent aboriginal collaboration as well as sensitive settlement assistance. Policy is real when documentation is embraced at enrolment systems, interpreters are normal, racism gets front and center and families are involved in decision making.

Strategies for Practice

1. Have qualified interpreters in consequential conversations and consent; do not assign children and siblings to translate sensitive information.
2. Have a cultural and language strengths discussion on enrolment, inquiring about names, languages, routines, family goals, celebrations and communication preferences.
3. Maintain home language with bilingual labels, songs, books and family stories and authorization of children to access all their language material in the play process.
4. Representation and bias audit curriculum, which provides year-round display of the cultural materials and does not feature costumes, stereotypes or tourist attitude to the culture.
5. Scaffold transition, using photos, visual plans, predictable routines and gradual family involvement especially in cases where children are unaccustomed to group-care arrangements.

Community and Professional Partnerships

1. TIS National interpreters facilitate proper two-way communication in facilitating enrolment, consent, health and safeguarding discussions.
2. SETS or Humanitarian Settlement Program workers can coordinate settlement, education and community participation needs with family consent.
3. Refugee health nurses have a chance to determine health, immunization and developmental needs and assist families in navigating health systems.
4. STARTTS or Foundation House trauma counsellors offer specialist advice when it comes to refugee experiences and may guide ECEC in engaging the trauma consciously.
5. Bicultural workers and community leaders can read between the lines, build up trust and co-design culturally legitimate strategies without having to represent all families.

Resources for Educators and Children

Programs and websites

1. TIS National: supports professional interpreting and language access for meaningful family participation.
2. Refugee Council of Australia: provides settlement information and educator context on refugee experiences without reducing families to trauma.
3. STARTTS: offers trauma-informed resources and referral pathways for refugee-background families in New South Wales.
4. Foundation House: provides Victorian refugee trauma support and professional resources useful for culturally responsive practice.

Children’s storybooks

1. My Two Blankets, Irena Kobald: explores language, friendship and belonging after migration through accessible metaphor.
2. I’m Australian Too, Mem Fox: represents multiple migration stories and invites discussion of belonging in contemporary Australia.
3. Whoever You Are, Mem Fox: highlights human commonality while educators also discuss meaningful cultural difference.
4. Welcome to Country, Aunty Joy Murphy and Lisa Kennedy: introduces connection to Country and supports respectful First Nations perspectives.

Videos and educational media

1. Little J & Big Cuz: centres Aboriginal children, family, Country and everyday learning rather than presenting culture as an “occasion”.
2. Play School cultural and language episodes: use songs and family stories to normalise varied languages and traditions.
3. ABC Kids family, community and culture resources: provide educator-guided media for discussing identity and belonging.
4. Sesame Workshop resources on belonging and racial justice: support age-appropriate conversations about fairness, identity and respectful difference.
