Context 5: Crisis and Emergency Context - Family Violence and Displacement

Understanding the Context

The situation of family violence is a crisis since it can render the primary caregiving environment of the child unpredictable as well as disrupt housing, finances, relationships and services access. This is not a fringe phenomenon as prevalence evidence indicates: 13 percent of adults were exposed to a partner being violent with a parent before the age of fifteen, and 27 percent of women were exposed to violence by an intimate partner or family member since the age of fifteen (Australian Bureau of Statistics [ABS], 2023). The Australian Child Maltreatment Study also reported that there was a prevalence of parent-parent exposure to domestic violence (Mathews et al., 2023). ECEC must thus be equipped to identify instances of distress and act safely without making educators investigators.

A model of ecological violence (Heise, 1998) is one that places risk at individual, relationship, community, and societal levels of gender inequality, economic dependence, housing insecurity and access to services. In children, developmental trauma perspectives can be used to explain how repetitively being threatened can modify the attention to danger, regulation and trust. It is also necessary that longitudinal evidence: Schulz et al. (2024) conducted a study indicating that exposure to intimate partner violence in infancy was a predictor of worse social skills, more peer problems and higher levels of peer victimization in ten years of age, even when prior exposure was controlled. However, resilience can be achieved when the children are provided with good caregivers who are responsive and safe and when they maintain good relationships outside the violence zone.

Impact on Children and Families

Young children can hear violence and witness injuries, be coercively controlled, indirectly, be used to threaten, move abruptly, lose objects or become disoriented with familiar people and places. This may manifest in ECEC as hypervigilance, aggression, freezing, regression, sleepiness, separation distress, controlling play or sudden changes in attendance. Educators ought to put behaviour into perspective without probing questions. The purpose of the service is to offer immediate relational safety, adhere to child-protection procedures and emergency, record factual observations, maintain confidentiality, and avoid behavior that might alert an individual with violence or raise risk.

Social Policy and Australian Responses

The National Plan to End Violence against Women and Children 20222032 (DSS, 2022) plans action by prevention, early intervention, response, and recovery and healing. Safe and Supported 20212031 (DSS, 2021) introduces a national child-protection framework centered around previous assistance and coordinated systems. ECEC requirements have also become more robust: Reforms to the National Quality Framework beginning in 20252026 have tightened the focus on child-safety, with the introduction of child-safety responsibilities in Quality Areas 2 and 7 (ACECQA, 2026b). These frameworks enhance accountability, but policy is solely protective in cases where services have a trained staff, up-to-date local referral pathways and safe information practices. Where digital monitoring, coercive control or homelessness can determine what may be safe contact, a generic contact support response is inappropriate.

Strategies for Practice

1. Have an up-to-date service safety policy including disclosure, documentation, child safety, emergency responses, privacy and secure communication with caregivers.
2. Co-regulate and respond to children with calmness and routine; do not punish threat-based behaviour and build limits again once safety is achieved.
3. When a child talks about harm, posing only open, necessary questions and accurately recording the words is preferable, and following reporting rules in the jurisdiction instead of investigating them.
4. Develop transition continuity plans when children are transferred to refuge, temporary housing or any other service by providing information only with consent or legal authority.
5. Educate body autonomy, consent, trusted adults and help-seeking using developmentally appropriate curriculum without implying that it is up to the child to prevent violence.

Community and Professional Partnerships

1. Risk assessment, safety planning and refuge referral can be conducted by the specialist domestic and family violence services.
2. State or territory child-protection authorities react to the statutory threshold levels of abuse or neglect.
3. Recovery, regulation and caregiver-child relationships are supported by trauma-informed psychologists or counsellors.
4. Housing and homelessness workers assist families with stabilization of accommodation following displacement crisis.
5. Protection orders, parenting, and coordinated safety planning can be facilitated by legal and family support professionals.

Resources for Educators and Children

Programs and websites

1. 1800RESPECT: national information and specialist counselling pathways; educators can use its professional resources and safe referral guidance.
2. NAPCAN: prevention resources supporting child-safe, respectful communities and educator conversations.
3. Australian Childhood Foundation: trauma-informed professional resources for understanding children’s behaviour after adversity.
4. Bravehearts: child-safety education resources addressing personal safety and help-seeking.

Children’s storybooks

1. Some Secrets Should Never Be Kept, Jayneen Sanders: distinguishes unsafe secrets and supports trusted-adult help-seeking.
2. My Body! What I Say Goes!, Jayneen Sanders: teaches body boundaries, consent language and safety networks.
3. Once I Was Very Very Scared, Chandra Ghosh Ippen: helps children recognise varied trauma responses through animals and recovery.
4. A Terrible Thing Happened, Margaret M. Holmes: supports indirect discussion of frightening experiences and safe adult support.

Videos and educational media

1. Bravehearts, Ditto’s Keep Safe Adventure: reinforces body ownership, warning signs and help-seeking through child-friendly repetition.
2. Sesame Workshop trauma and “Comfy-Cozy Nest” resources: support calming, connection and caregiver-child regulation after frightening experiences.
3. Daniel Tiger, Grown-Ups Come Back: supports separation reassurance where safe and appropriate, while educators avoid applying it to unsafe caregivers.
4. Sesame Workshop emotional-wellbeing videos: model naming big feelings, breathing and seeking trusted support after stressful events.
