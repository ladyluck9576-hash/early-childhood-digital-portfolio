Crisis & Emergency Context

Family Violence & Displacement

Family violence can destabilise the child’s caregiving environment, housing, finances, relationships and access to services. ECEC responses must centre immediate safety, factual documentation, confidentiality and developmentally appropriate support.

13%of adults reported witnessing violence towards a parent by a partner before age fifteen (ABS, 2023).

The educator’s role is not to investigate. It is to recognise concerning patterns, respond safely and follow child-protection and emergency procedures.

Understanding the Context

Family violence is a crisis context because it can make the child’s primary caregiving environment unpredictable while also destabilising housing, finances, relationships and access to services. Prevalence evidence shows this is not marginal: 13% of adults reported witnessing violence towards a parent by a partner before age fifteen, and 27% of women reported violence by an intimate partner or family member since age fifteen (Australian Bureau of Statistics [ABS], 2023). The Australian Child Maltreatment Study likewise found widespread exposure to domestic violence between parents (Mathews et al., 2023). ECEC therefore needs competence in recognising patterns of distress and responding safely without turning educators into investigators.

An ecological model of violence (Heise, 1998) locates risk across individual, relationship, community and societal levels, including gender inequality, economic dependence, housing insecurity and service accessibility. Developmental trauma perspectives explain how repeated threat can alter attention toward danger, regulation and trust. Longitudinal evidence is especially important: Schulz et al. (2024) found that intimate partner violence exposure during infancy predicted lower social skills, more peer problems and greater peer victimisation at age ten, even after accounting for later exposure. Resilience remains possible where children have safe, responsive caregivers and stable relationships outside the violent environment.

Impact on Children & Families

Young children may hear violence, see injuries, experience coercive control indirectly, be used in threats, move suddenly, lose possessions or become separated from familiar people and places. In ECEC this can appear as hypervigilance, aggression, freezing, regression, sleepiness, separation distress, controlling play or abrupt attendance changes.

Educators should interpret behaviour in context without asking leading questions. The service’s role is to provide immediate relational safety, follow child-protection and emergency procedures, document factual observations, preserve confidentiality, and avoid actions that could alert a person using violence or increase risk.

Social Policy and Australian Responses

The National Plan to End Violence against Women and Children 2022–2032 (DSS, 2022) organises action across prevention, early intervention, response, and recovery and healing. Safe and Supported 2021–2031 (DSS, 2021) adds a national child-protection framework focused on earlier help and coordinated systems. National Quality Framework reforms commencing across 2025–2026 sharpen child-safety expectations, including explicit responsibilities within Quality Areas 2 and 7 (ACECQA, 2026b).

These frameworks improve accountability, but policy is only protective when services have trained staff, current local referral pathways and safe information practices. A generic “contact support” response is inadequate where digital monitoring, coercive control or homelessness may determine what contact is safe.

Five Evidence-Based Strategies for Practice

1Maintain a safety protocol
Cover disclosure, documentation, child protection, emergency response, privacy and safe communication with caregivers.
2Co-regulate calmly
Use predictable routines and avoid punitive discipline for threat-based behaviour; restore limits after safety is established.
3Ask only necessary questions
Use open questions, record children’s words accurately and follow reporting requirements rather than investigating.
4Plan continuity after displacement
Support transitions to refuge, temporary housing or another service, sharing information only with consent or lawful authority.
5Teach body autonomy
Use developmentally appropriate curriculum on consent, trusted adults and help-seeking without making children responsible for stopping violence.

Community & Professional Partnerships

Domestic & family violence services
Specialist risk assessment, safety planning and refuge referral.
Child-protection authorities
Respond when statutory thresholds for abuse or neglect are met.
Trauma-informed clinicians
Support recovery, regulation and caregiver-child relationships.
Housing & homelessness workers
Stabilise accommodation after crisis displacement.
Legal & family-support professionals
Assist with protection orders, parenting arrangements and coordinated safety planning.
